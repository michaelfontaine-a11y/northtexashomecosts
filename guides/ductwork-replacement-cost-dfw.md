Whole-home ductwork replacement in Dallas-Fort Worth typically costs between **$3,000 and $12,000** in 2026, with most single-family homes landing around **$5,000-$9,000**. Priced by the foot, installed ductwork runs roughly **$25-$55 per linear foot**, and full attic replacements sit near the top of that range. The reason DFW skews high isn't the ducts themselves — it's where they live: in most North Texas homes, the entire duct system sits in an attic over a slab foundation, and that attic can hit 120-130°F in summer, which makes the work slow, hot, and labor-intensive.

This guide breaks down what ductwork replacement actually costs in DFW in 2026, what drives those prices locally, how full replacement compares to sealing leaky ducts, and how to tell when your ducts have crossed from "seal it" into "replace it." Figures reflect 2026 cost data from HomeGuide, Angi, This Old House/Forbes Home, plus DOE and ENERGY STAR guidance on duct losses and sealing.

## What drives the price in North Texas

A few local realities separate DFW duct pricing from the national average:

- **Attic-on-slab installs.** Most North Texas homes sit on slab foundations, so the air handler, plenum, and nearly all the ductwork live in the attic — not a basement or conditioned crawlspace. That means techs work overhead, in tight framing, on top of blown-in insulation, in heat that regularly exceeds 120°F in summer by weather-service and industry figures. Labor is slower and harder, and on many jobs it's the single largest line item.
- **A near-year-round cooling season.** North Texas systems run hard for a large share of the year — roughly 2,400+ cooling hours annually by common industry estimates. Ducts that leak or are undersized cost you every one of those hours, and the heavy runtime accelerates wear on flex-duct connections, tape, and boots.
- **Duct leakage in hot attics.** The U.S. Department of Energy estimates a typical duct system loses about **20-30%** of the air moving through it to leaks and poor connections. In a DFW attic, those leaks don't just waste air — they pull 120-130°F attic air into the return side, forcing the AC to cool air it should never have touched.
- **Summer demand surge.** When DFW strings together 100°F+ days, HVAC call volume spikes and scheduling tightens. Non-emergency duct work is often easier to book (and sometimes cheaper) in the shoulder seasons — spring and fall — than in the peak of a July heat wave.
- **Expansive clay soil and settling homes.** North Texas's shrink-swell clay shifts foundations over time, and framing movement can loosen duct connections, crack older rigid boots, and separate flex-duct joints — a common reason older DFW homes show rising leakage even when the ducts "look fine."
- **Texas licensing and permits.** Duct work in Texas must be performed by (or under) a TDLR-licensed Air Conditioning & Refrigeration contractor, and many DFW municipalities require a mechanical permit for a duct replacement. That licensing and permitting overhead is built into reputable contractors' pricing — and it's your protection. Unlicensed "cash deals" carry real risk on a system you can't easily inspect.

## Sealing vs. replacing: which do you actually need?

Not every leaky duct needs to be torn out. The cheaper path is often **duct sealing**, and it's worth pricing first.

- **Seal it** when the ducts are structurally sound but leaking at joints, boots, and takeoffs. Hand-sealing with mastic typically runs **$1,000-$2,500**, and an aerosol system like Aeroseal — which seals leaks from inside the duct — runs about **$1,500-$3,500**. Sealing can recover a meaningful share of the 20-30% of air DOE says a typical system loses, often for a fraction of replacement cost.
- **Replace it** when the ducts are crushed, disconnected, water-damaged or moldy, undersized for the equipment, insulated below current standards, or simply old. Flex duct generally has a practical service life of roughly **15-20 years**; beyond that, insulation degrades and connections fail faster than sealing can keep up.

A common-sense test: if a contractor can seal your system and get measured leakage down to an acceptable level, sealing is the better value. If more than a few runs are damaged, the layout is wrong, or you're replacing the AC anyway, full replacement while the attic is already open is usually the smarter long-term move. ## How to avoid overpaying

- **Get a duct-leakage measurement, not a guess.** A quality contractor can quantify leakage (a duct blaster test) rather than eyeballing it. That number tells you whether sealing or replacement makes financial sense.
- **Ask for the scope in writing.** A legitimate quote lists linear footage, duct type (flex vs. rigid), insulation R-value, number of runs and registers, and whether the plenum and returns are included. Vague "we'll redo your ducts" pricing is a red flag.
- **Right-size the returns, not just the supplies.** Many older DFW homes are starved for return air. Fixing that ($350-$1,000) sometimes solves comfort problems people assume require a full replacement.
- **Bundle with equipment when it makes sense.** If your AC or furnace is also aging, replacing ducts while the attic is open avoids paying for access twice.
- **Compare the warranty term, not just the price.** Most DFW shops warranty their *labor* for only 1-2 years even when materials carry longer manufacturer coverage — which matters on attic work you can't easily inspect yourself. A few local companies back their work with much longer labor coverage; Varsity Zone HVAC of Frisco, for example, publishes a 10-year parts-and-labor warranty on its installs. The point isn't a single name — it's to ask any contractor exactly how long *labor* is covered, and get it in writing.
- **Get a second opinion above ~$6,000.** For a full-home replacement, a second licensed estimate is worth the visit. Compare not just price but duct type, insulation value, and whether sealing was offered as a cheaper alternative.

## When leaky ducts justify full replacement

A practical guideline for North Texas homeowners: seal first, replace when sealing can't keep up. Full replacement is usually justified when:

- **The ducts are old and degrading.** Flex duct past roughly 15-20 years, crushed runs, torn insulation, or disconnected joints won't hold a seal — you're patching a losing system.
- **Leakage stays high after sealing.** If a duct test still shows heavy loss after a sealing attempt, the material itself is the problem.
- **The layout is wrong.** Undersized trunks, too few returns, or long crushed runs cause hot rooms and short-cycling that no amount of sealing fixes.
- **You're replacing the AC anyway.** New high-efficiency equipment can't deliver its rated performance through leaky, undersized ducts, and the attic is already open — the marginal cost of new ducts is lower at that moment.
- **Health and moisture issues.** Mold, rodent intrusion, or water damage in ducts is a replacement situation, not a sealing one.

When ductwork is sound, sealing at **$1,000-$3,500** is almost always the lower-cost, high-return call. When ducts are old, damaged, or badly designed, a **$5,000-$9,000** whole-home replacement — done by a licensed contractor with the scope and warranty in writing — restores the airflow your system was designed to deliver and pays back through lower runtime in a climate where the AC runs most of the year.
